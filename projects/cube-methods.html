CUBE METHODS

There are hundreds of ways to solve a Rubik's Cube. These are the four that actually matter — what each one does, how it works, and which one you should learn.

Before The Methods

The vocabulary. This takes five minutes and it makes everything after it click.

Every method in this guide is a sequence of stages. You finish stage one completely, then stage two, then stage three, and so on until the cube is solved. The four methods differ almost entirely in what those stages are and how much you have to memorise.

Two facts explain the whole landscape. A cube has 43,252,003,274,489,856,000 possible arrangements and exactly one solved state — and you can always get there, because the pieces can never escape. And you only ever need 18 of the 20 moves to solve anything; the other two (x and z) exist for writing puzzle positions down, not for solving.

The six faces

Hold the cube with a corner facing you and a flat face on top. That gives you six faces, and every move is named after one of them.

U
UpThe top face. Turn it like a dial to the right.
D
DownThe bottom face. Same motion, underneath.
R
RightThe face on the right. Turn it away from you and down.
L
LeftThe face on the left. Turn it away from you and up.
F
FrontThe face pointing at you. Turn it like a door opening left.
B
BackThe face pointing away. Turn it like a door opening right.

An apostrophe means "the other way"

So R and R' cancel each other out instantly, and R R is the same as R2 — a half turn. Read any algorithm left to right, and never put the cube down in the middle of one.

The two numbers you'll hear constantly

  • 78 — the number of algorithms in the official CFOP last layer: 21 to permute the pieces, 57 to twist them. Every other method here exists partly to beat that number by fusing the two stages together.
  • 42 — the number of CMLL cases used by Roux. The same last-layer goal as CFOP, solved in one stage, which is why Roux needs so few algorithms.

The two ideas that make it click

  1. Everything happens in order. You cannot work on the top layer while the bottom two are a mess, and every method exists because of this constraint. Each stage hands a cleaner cube to the next one.
  2. Some piece swaps are impossible on their own. Two corners can never be swapped alone. Two edges can never be swapped alone. A cube can look "impossible" and still be one move away from being solvable — you just have to move a third piece out of the way and bring it back. This is exactly why the last layer is the fiddly part of every single method.

#4 · THE ON-RAMP

BEGINNER

Layer by Layer (LBL) — the default first method for over forty years

Solve one layer at a time, top to bottom, and never take a piece back out of a layer you've already finished. It's by far the easiest method to learn and it gets you to a solved cube in an afternoon.

Seven stages, but they collapse into three ideas: solve the bottom cross, insert things into it, then fix the top layer. Stages 4–7 are literally the OLL and PLL stages of CFOP, just done in beginner-friendly pieces.

1The white cross

Put white on the bottom and turn the whole cube until a white edge is at the front, white facing you. Do the same for all four, and while you're at it get the side colours matching. That last requirement is the thing beginners skip, and it matters for every stage after this one — if white sits under green, the corner you build later will have green facing the wrong way and you'll have to take it back out.

Two rules make the cross almost free. If an edge is in the top layer and can drop straight into its slot, hold it above that slot and turn F twice. If a yellow face is pointing at you in the top row, walk it around with F and U until its white sticker faces up, line it up, then drop it. If you get lost, just repeat those two patterns — they cover everything. The cross is the one stage where the beginner method is genuinely as fast as CFOP.

2The white corners

One corner at a time, and here's the trick that makes it foolproof: never rotate the cube in your hands. Instead, find a white corner in the top layer and turn the top face U until that corner is sitting directly above the empty slot it belongs in. If its white sticker is facing you, turn that slot's face R or L once to tip it in. If the white sticker is facing the side, keep turning U until it's facing you, and do the same thing.

You will end up inserting the same corner three times, from three different angles, before all four are in. That is completely normal and it is the entire method. The insertion itself is a single algorithm per hand, and its mirror handles the other side.

Insert corner · right hand
R U R'
The classic insertion trigger. Repeat it with a U turn between tries until the corner drops in.
Insert corner · left hand
L U L' U L U L'
The exact mirror of the one above. You'll type this from memory, not from sight.
Insert edge · right slot
U R U' R' U' F' U F
Nine moves. The F2L "disguised sexy move" — the same idea CFOP uses.
Insert edge · left slot
U' L U L' U' F U F'
Nine moves, mirrored. Middle edges can't drop in like cross edges can — this is why they need a sequence.

3Orienting the top face

Two separate goals now: get all nine yellow stickers facing up, then get the pieces into the right places. The order is deliberate — face first, position second, and never the other way around.

For the yellow cross, a single F R F puts one more edge in, and the mirrored version handles the other side. For the face, if you have an L-shape of yellow on top then one Sune from the right position solves the whole face. A dot takes three of them, a line takes two. Sune is one of the 57 OLL cases in CFOP — you're already learning it.

Now the top is all yellow but the pieces are scrambled. Turn U until two yellow corners are correctly placed against the right and back faces, then stop turning the top layer and hold the cube still. If no two corners are right you have an H or U shape; R U R U U F R U R U F converts it into a findable one, and you go back to hunting for your two corners. This stage feels like cheating because it is: you're using a big move to fix a shape so a small rule can apply.

Sune — the famous one
R U R' U R U R'
Orients the last face. The single most recognised algorithm in the sport.
Anti-Sune
R U R' U' R' F R F'
The other side of the same family. Together with Sune they cover half of 2-look OLL.
H-perm shape fix

A real PLL: swaps two pairs of corners and two pairs of edges. Nine moves, no setup needed.
Y-perm — the last one
F R U' R' U' R U R' F' R U R' U' R' F R F'
Swaps two corners and two edges diagonally. The beginner method's hardest sequence, and the only genuinely awkward one.

The beginner method gets dismissed as a waste of time because it uses more moves and takes longer. It isn't. Roughly 120 moves executed deliberately is genuinely easier than 55 moves executed while recognising cases, planning F2L, and looking ahead. Most people who "waste" a month on the beginner method reach CFOP faster than people who skip it and burn out — because they arrived with working hands and full command of the notation.

#1 · THE STANDARD

CFOP

Cross · F2L · OLL · PLL — named after its stages, popularised by Jessica Fridrich in 1997

The method the World Cube Association recognises, the one the world records are set with, and the one roughly three in five speedcubers use. It is the beginner method with the middle and the top collapsed into fewer, faster decisions.

Four stages. That is the entire method. Everything below is just detail on how each stage gets optimised — and the single biggest jump is stage one, where beginner's four separate decisions become one planned sequence.

1Cross — the stage that actually decides your time

Beginner cross is four separate problems. CFOP cross is one. Before touching the cube you look at the four edges that belong on the bottom and work out an order plus a set of moves that solves all four without disturbing the others. The result is a solid bottom layer with correctly aligned sides.

The huge leap is X-Cross: solving the cross and the back-right F2L pair in one unbroken sequence, chosen during inspection. It feels like cheating, and it's why the best CFOP solvers look like they barely move during the first stage — they've already decided everything and they're just typing it in. Nobody is fast at the cross. They're just early.

2F2L — where all the actual work lives

F2L means "first two layers". You're placing a corner and an edge into the bottom two layers so they end up as a matched, correctly oriented pair. There are 41 algorithms because each pair has many possible states — but almost all of them resolve to the same three cases once you've executed R U R' U R U R' to pair them up first.

This is the idea that trips up every new speedcuber: you are allowed to make a piece look wrong on purpose. "This" is not a problem to solve, it's a setup to be undone by a trigger. Once that clicks, F2L stops being memorisation and starts being three ideas you'll never need a table for again. The sexy move — six turns, R U R' U' — does most of the work.

3OLL and PLL — the 78

OLL is Orientation of the Last Layer: all nine top stickers facing up, in any arrangement. There are 57 cases, but they fall into two families — 3 corner cases and 54 edge cases, and those 54 are really just 7 algorithms applied in different setups. 2-look OLL does both families in sequence for a total of 10 algorithms.

PLL is Permutation of the Last Layer: everything oriented, now put the pieces where they belong. There are 21 cases, splitting into corner permutations, edge permutations, and mixed cases. 2-look PLL handles corners first then edges using 7 algorithms and a few setup moves.

This is the deal CFOP offers: 17 algorithms plus a little thinking gets you a solid mid-teens average, and all 78 gets you to the front of the pack. Nobody needs all 78 on day one, and pretending otherwise is how people quit.

Learn 2-look OLL and 2-look PLL first — 17 algorithms, total. Get your average under 30 seconds with those, then replace them piece by piece: full PLL is a weekend, full OLL is a month. That progression is the entire difference between "I gave up on speedcubing" and "I average 15 seconds".

WHY EVERYONE STICKS WITH IT
  • Resources. Fifteen years of tutorials, technique development, and documented improvements you can copy.
  • Low floor, enormous ceiling. 60-second solvers and 6-second solvers both use it. You can stop improving at literally any speed.
  • Big cubes. The only one of these four with a proven 6×6 and 7×7 method at world-record level (Yau).
  • It scales down. A 30-second CFOP solver and a 6-second CFOP solver run the same four stages.
WHERE IT COSTS YOU
  • Move count. Around 55 moves — you have to be fast to overcome the inefficiency.
  • Two-handed bias. All that R and U turning is awkward one-handed, and it's why the OH records aren't CFOP.
  • 78 algorithms. More memorising than anything else in this guide except ZZ's full last layer.
  • Rotation. Big-cube Yau needs the whole cube turned constantly.
#2 · THE INTUITIVE ONE

ROUX

Hervé Roux — released 1995, named after its inventor

Build blocks instead of filling layers, never turn the cube around, and learn the fewest algorithms of any method in this guide. Second-most-popular method in the world, and the best one for solving with one hand.

Notice the two zeros. The first two stages of Roux — which is most of the solve, most of the thinking, and all of the difficulty — need no algorithms at all. It's blockbuilding: visual, logical, and learnable without a single table of sequences.

1First block — the only stage that really hurts

Roux starts by building a solid 2×2×3 block anywhere in the cube. You can build it at the front-left, front-right, back-left or back-right — whatever the scramble makes easiest. The block is four pieces (three corners and one edge) forming one corner of the cube, and it must be solid with matching colours on the outside.

This is the one genuinely hard stage of Roux, and the honest truth is that it's hard for everybody. The skill it builds — holding a partial block in your head while you insert a piece into it — is the defining skill of the whole method. It's also why Roux solvers develop a distinctive way of holding the cube and look faintly lost halfway through a solve: they're always thinking.

2Pairs, and the 1:2 trick

Six corner+edge pairs have to go into the middle layer. A pair counts as solved when the corner and edge are together and correctly oriented relative to each other — but the pair can be sitting anywhere in the cube, because rotating it into position at the end is free.

Then comes the idea that makes Roux efficient: 1:2 pairing. Find a piece that belongs in the first block and use it as the first piece of a new block on top. While that block is standing there, insert the piece that completes it. Now two pairs are done. Lift the whole 2×2×3 block out of the way in a single move, and you pick up a free solve. This is where the ~45 move count comes from — Roux manufactures efficiency by building structure, not by hunting for shorter algorithms.

3LSE — the last six edges

After the pairs, exactly six edges are unsolved. LSE does three jobs at once: edge orientation (flip the cube and solve the bottom cross — the first time orientation is resolved in this method), placing the E-slice edges, and getting the last two corners into a workable state for CMLL.

Orientation is deliberately delayed. Roux is free to shuffle edges around for three whole stages because the 2×2×2 of loose corners gives it enough freedom, and then it resolves everything in one go. This is Roux's signature trick: delay, then collapse. ZZ runs the same idea but resolves it in two moves at the very start instead of at the very end.

4CMLL — 42 algorithms and you're done

CMLL is Corners and Middle-layer Last Layer. It orients and permutes the three remaining corners and the three E-slice edges in a single stage, finishing the cube. There are 42 cases rather than 78 because it never separates orientation from permutation the way OLL-then-PLL does — it does both at once, so there are fewer total cases.

After CMLL the last two corners are already oriented and only need a U2 to place. And the whole finish is mostly M and U moves — the fastest, most ergonomic turns on any cube. Roux solvers routinely finish a solve with a blur of the hand.

The sledge
R' F R F'

A wide move disguised as a slice move, which hides a U turn. Used constantly, costs almost nothing.

WHY PEOPLE LOVE IT
  • Fewest algorithms. 42, against CFOP's 78. That is the whole memorisation burden.
  • Lowest move count of any method in this guide, around 45 moves.
  • No rotation. The cube never leaves your hands facing the same way — the only genuinely rotationless method here.
  • One-handed. The best one-handed times ever set are Roux. Kian Mansour has averaged under 9 seconds one-handed.
WHY PEOPLE QUIT
  • The first block. Genuinely hard to learn, and no shortcut exists.
  • You think during the solve. Blockbuilding can't be executed on autopilot, which caps turning speed.
  • CMLL is a wall. 42 cases with few visual cues — you have to recognise them fast, by feel.
  • Thinner resources. Far fewer years of collective development than CFOP has had.
#3 · THE ROTATIONLESS ONE

ZZ

Zbigniew Zborowski — released 2011, also called ZZM or the Zouma method

Orient every edge in the entire cube before you touch anything else, and never rotate the cube again. It's the youngest method here, the least popular, and the one that currently owns the world record.

ZZ has the shortest first stage in competitive cubing — the best solvers finish edge orientation before they've finished looking at the cube. Every stage after it is a downstream consequence of that single decision.

1Edge orientation — the whole idea

Here is the observation the entire method rests on: a cube has an edge-flip parity. You cannot flip one edge on its own, but you can flip three, or five. There are exactly 12 legal ways to orient twelve edges, which is precisely why this stage is winnable by a human at all.

ZZ resolves this first, using only R, U, L and D moves. Those four faces never turn the cube around, so the moment orientation is done, the cube is already facing the right way for everything that follows. Two further consequences fall out for free:

  • No rotations, ever. The cube is solved in one fixed position, which is why ZZ is strong one-handed and the only method here with a proven rotationless big-cube path.
  • A much easier F2L. With all twelve edges oriented, the awkward last-layer edge cases collapse. That is where the move-count advantage comes from.

The trade is brutal and worth stating plainly: EO is the hardest single stage in any method in this guide. It uses faces your fingers hate, it has no visual anchor to hold onto, and the strong versions require planning a large search during inspection. Top ZZ solvers are famous for finishing EO before they finish looking at the cube. For most people this stage is the entire reason not to use ZZ, and that is a perfectly good reason.

2Two ways to start the rest

EOline solves just the DF and DB edges after orientation finishes. It uses fewer moves and feeds straight into blockbuilding, and it's the classic ZZ setup. EOcross solves the entire bottom cross as part of orientation instead. It costs more moves but is far more comfortable to execute and allows much more lookahead — and most modern ZZ solvers have drifted toward it, because at high turning speed the efficiency isn't worth the awkwardness.

3F2L, with orientation already solved

Identical goal to every other method here: solve the bottom two layers. But because all twelve edges are already oriented, the standard F2L awkward cases simply don't exist. A corner+edge pair that would need a workaround in CFOP is just insertable. This is the concrete payoff of doing orientation first, and it's why a well-executed ZZ solve averages around 45 moves.

4The last layer, and the 493 problem

You now choose exactly how much you want to memorise. Because orientation is already solved, the last layer is dramatically easier than CFOP's two stages — but "easier" and "small" are not the same thing.

  • OCLL + PLL — 28 algorithms. Corner orientation, then full permutation. This is the sensible place to start and plenty fast.
  • ZBLL — 493 algorithms. Orients and permutes the last layer in one stage by solving the last F2L pair at the same time. It's the best finish available to a human being, and it's why most of the top ten competitors in the world now use some form of ZZ — including the holders of both the 3×3 average and 3×3 single world records.

WHY IT MATTERS
  • It owns the record. Both the 3×3 average and the 3×3 single world records are currently held by ZZ-based methods.
  • True rotationless solving. The only method here where the cube never changes hands or orientation, start to finish.
  • Real mathematical structure. EO is a small, closed, finite problem, and people genuinely enjoy it.
  • Best one-handed times outside Roux. Fewer moves, and the M-slice work suits a single hand.
WHY IT'S LAST
  • 493 algorithms for the top-level version — the largest memorisation burden in this guide by a factor of six.
  • EO is brutal to learn and much harder to practise than anything else here.
  • You do it or you don't. There's no useful halfway — a partial EO isn't a slower ZZ, it's a broken one.
  • Almost no beginner resources. You'll be largely on your own for the first month.

So Which One?

An honest decision guide

There is a right answer here and it is almost always CFOP. Not because it is the most efficient method — it isn't — but because it has the lowest skill floor and the highest ceiling of anything in this guide, plus the resources to carry you from one to the other. Everything else is a personality match.

If you…Then learnBecause
Have never solved oneBeginnerNon-negotiable. Every method here assumes you can already solve a cube slowly.

Don't pick a method by looking at world records. The world's best CFOP solver is not the world's best solver, and a method you quit in month two teaches you nothing. Look at people a year ahead of you, pick the one whose thinking sounds like fun — recognition-based or construction-based — and stay there long enough to genuinely get good. Switching methods at 45 seconds to chase a theoretically faster one is the single most common way people quit the hobby.

A Realistic Timeline

What actually happens, stage by stage

WEEK 1
Learn the moves, then the beginner method
Do twenty slow solves a day. Yes, twenty. The bottleneck is your hands reading notation, not your brain understanding the cube. Expect 2:00–5:00 times. This stage feels insultingly slow and that is completely normal.
WEEK 2 – 4
Automate, then start learning CFOP thinking
Get beginner reliably under 1:30 with zero thinking. Then stop using the beginner method entirely and start CFOP: cross planning first, then F2L. Do not try to learn all 41 F2L cases at once — learn the six most common and look the rest up.
MONTH 2 – 3
2-look OLL and 2-look PLL
Seventeen algorithms. Sub-30 is realistic right here. This is the highest-value stretch of the whole progression, and it's the one most people skip in favour of grinding F2L for two extra months.
MONTH 4 – 6
Full PLL, then full OLL
21 PLL algorithms is a weekend. 57 OLL is a month of daily drilling. Sub-15 is roughly where you'll land. Add X-Cross and F2L lookahead once you're consistently under 20 seconds.
MONTH 6 AND ONWARD
Pick your ceiling
Stay on CFOP and chase sub-10 with better technique, or switch to Roux or ZZ for lower move counts. Switching is easiest now, because you already solve a cube without thinking about the method — you're replacing stages, not learning to solve.

Three drills that actually work

  • Cross planning, ten minutes a day. Look at a scramble, plan a full cross, write it down, then execute it. Two months of this is worth more than any algorithm set — the cross is the stage where planning pays off most.
  • Algorithm review on a timer. Twenty-one PLL algorithms executed as fast as you can with no thinking at all. Recognition has to become reflex, and reflex only comes from volume.
  • Deliberately awkward practice. Solve with your non-dominant hand, or solve while refusing to rotate the cube. Sounds punitive. It's the fastest way to find out whether your real speed is finger speed or thinking speed — and it's usually the latter.

The Thing Nobody Tells You

A short note to finish on

Every method in this guide is a way of organising attention. Beginner lets you think about almost nothing, which is precisely why it's relaxing. CFOP asks you to recognise things and recall strings. Roux and ZZ ask you to construct things in your head. Speedcubing isn't really about moving your fingers quickly — the world's best solvers sit motionless for two full seconds at the start of a solve, planning, and then the cube simply collapses into itself.

Pick the method that matches how you already like thinking. The people still at this after ten years aren't the ones who learned the most algorithms. They're the ones who found a way of looking at a scrambled cube that felt like a problem they wanted to solve again the next morning.

Start here, not there

Beginner method first, always. Skipping it doesn't save time — it just means you learn the notation and the cube at the same time, and both suffer.

Write the sequences out

Memorising algorithms by watching videos does not work. Write each one out ten times, execute it fifty times, then write it again. The hand remembers what the eye only sees.

Slow is a stage, not a failure

Everyone's first two hundred solves are slow. The people who improve are the ones who did solve two hundred times before deciding it wasn't for them.
